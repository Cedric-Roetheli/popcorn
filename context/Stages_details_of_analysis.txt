Stage 3 — Role assignment (optional, framing-dependent)
Goal: For each character extracted in Stage 2 (Protagonist, Antagonist, up to 3 Additional), assign at most one narrative role—only if the portrayal clearly fits—otherwise return none.
Role set (pick one or none)
	• hero — portrayed as trying to resolve the central conflict or protect others; the narrative signals approval or sympathy for their actions.
	• villain — portrayed as creating/sustaining the central conflict or causing harm/obstruction; the narrative signals disapproval or blame.
	• victim — portrayed as suffering harm or threat with limited agency relative to the central conflict.
	• ambiguous — portrayal mixes cues (e.g., anti-hero), no clear endorsement or condemnation.
	• none — not enough evidence in the provided text to assign a role relative to the central conflict.
	Framing principle: Roles reflect portrayal and narrative viewpoint, not legal/moral labels.
	Example: If Peter steals from Bob’s shop to feed Ashley, Peter can be framed as hero (altruistic, endorsed) or villain (harmful, condemned); Bob or Ashley may be victim depending on how the story frames harm and sympathy.
How to decide (use only provided text)
	1. Anchor to the central conflict from Stage 1.
	2. Look for framing cues: evaluative language (brave/cruel), who gets sympathy/support, who inflicts/absorbs harm, who solves problems, resolution/outcomes.
	3. If cues conflict or are weak, choose ambiguous or none rather than guessing.
Guardrails
	• Do not infer from outside knowledge or actor reputations.
	• Do not use gender/race to influence the role decision.
	• Provide an evidence quote (≤25 words) from the supplied text for every non-none assignment; for none, provide a brief reason (“insufficient evidence in text”).

Stage 4 — Moral framing
(Universalism vs. Particularism for characters and film; Deontological vs. Consequentialist for heroes only)
Use only the supplied plot/subtitle text. If unclear, return none and explain briefly. Provide evidence quotes.
Definitions (brief)
	• Universalism: justifies actions with principles that apply to everyone (equal rules/rights).
	• Particularism: justifies actions with exceptions based on group/relationship/status.
	• Deontological: duty/rules/rights-based reasoning.
	• Consequentialist: outcomes/trade-offs/“greater good” reasoning.
Who to score
	• Universalism–Particularism (UP): score each Stage-2 character (Protagonist, Antagonist, up to 3 Additional) and a film-overall summary.
	• Deontological–Consequentialist (DC): score only characters whose Stage-3 role = hero. For others, return none.
